The money decisions

Six calls to make before launch, the numbers behind each one, and the risks that can actually take you down when there's only one of you. Two figures currently on your website are wrong — that's first.

Your pricing page compares you against “a typical 8% platform”, and BUSINESS_MODEL.md says Tix.Africa charges 8% + ₦100. That number came from your own repo and I put it on the site without checking it. It's wrong.

Fix this before anyone sees it

Overstating a competitor's price on your own marketing page is the kind of thing that gets screenshotted. It's also just inaccurate, and your real numbers are good enough without it.

02 — POSITIONING

You're selling to the wrong events

A flat fee is a discount for expensive tickets and a premium for cheap ones. That's not a flaw in the model, it's the whole shape of it — and it decides who you should be chasing.

Nigerian ticket prices span roughly 75× — a ₦2,000 club night to a ₦150,000 weekend retreat. No single flat number sits sensibly across that range.

Who you should be selling to

  • Conferences — ₦25,000–₦80,000, hundreds of delegates
  • Retreats and workshops — ₦100,000+, small rooms, high value
  • Festivals with VIP tiers — where the ₦120,000 table is the money
  • Corporate and paid networking — priced high, run by people who count

Who you shouldn't lead with

Club nights and ₦2,000-a-head parties. You'll win them on the door scanner and on getting paid the same night — but never on price, because there's nothing to win.

Recommendation

Keep the flat-fee pitch, but aim the marketing at higher-value ticketing. “Never a percentage” is a much louder promise to someone selling a ₦60,000 conference seat than to someone selling ₦2,000 entry.

03 — PRICING

₦200 flat leaves most of your money on the table

At ₦200 a ticket you earn the same from a ₦150,000 retreat seat as from a ₦2,000 rave. On the retreat that's 0.13% — you're practically working for free on exactly the events where you're most valuable.

The fix: bands, not a percentage

Keep “a flat fee per ticket, never a percentage of your revenue” — it's still completely true. Just have more than one flat fee.

04 — THE FEE YOU FORGOT

Paystack takes more than you do

There's a second fee on every sale and it isn't yours. Paystack charges 1.5% + ₦100 on Nigerian cards, capped at ₦2,000 — plus 7.5% VAT on that fee. Tickets at or under ₦2,500 are processed free.

Right now your code makes the organiser absorb this, which is the normal arrangement — but it means their real cost is your fee plus Paystack's, and they'll judge you on the total. On a ₦20,000 ticket that's ₦630, not ₦200.

Say it out loud on your pricing page

Every competitor buries this. Showing the full cost — “₦200 to us, about ₦430 to the bank, ₦19,370 to you” — would be the most trustworthy pricing page in the market, and it costs you nothing because everyone pays the processor.

05 — YOUR REAL WEAPON

You pay out instantly. Tix pays weekly.

This is the thing I'd build the whole company around, and it's currently buried on your site as a footnote about not holding money.

Tix settles organisers weekly. Your split payments land the organiser's share in their own bank at the moment of sale. For someone paying a venue deposit, a sound guy and a caterer before the doors open, that is not a technical detail — it's working capital.

Recommendation

Lead with this alongside the fee. “Your money the same day, not next week” beats a fee comparison for anyone who has ever had to pay a venue before an event. It's also very hard for a competitor holding funds to copy, because their float is how they operate.

06 — RISK

What can actually take you down

The no-custody model is your best pitch and your biggest exposure. The money is gone the moment it arrives — which is great until something needs reversing.

Chargebacks are the one that ends companies

A buyer disputes a ₦200,000 table two months after the event. The money is long since in the organiser's bank. Paystack takes it back from the merchant account — and the platform is usually the merchant of record. You'd be paying it out of your own pocket, with no float, chasing an organiser who has already spent it.

Find out before you launch

Ask Paystack directly, in writing, who bears a chargeback on a split transaction — you or the subaccount. The answer changes your entire risk profile, and it's a ten-minute email. Do this one first.

Fake events

Someone lists an event that doesn't exist, sells 400 tickets, the money settles instantly to their own account, and they disappear. You cannot claw it back — that's what no-custody means. The buyers will come to you.

Mitigation: hold new organisers to a lower limit until they've run one event cleanly, and verify identity before allowing paid events above a threshold.

You are the on-call engineer, on a Saturday night

Events happen at 10pm on weekends. If the door scanner fails with 300 people queuing, you are the support team, and there is no one to escalate to. Set expectations publicly — and make sure the manual-code fallback is something you'd trust with a real queue, because it's your safety net.

Refunds

The organiser has the money, so only they can refund. Buyers won't see it that way — they bought from your site. Put refund policy on the checkout page in plain words, before someone pays.

09 — WHAT I NEED FROM YOU

Six decisions

  1. Email Paystack about chargebacks Who pays when a split transaction is disputed — you or the organiser? Everything else waits on this answer.
  2. Flat ₦200, or four bands? Bands roughly double your revenue and keep the promise intact. My recommendation is bands.
  3. Should organisers be able to pass the fee to buyers? Tix already offers this, so it's table stakes rather than an edge. Small build.
  4. Aim upmarket, or stay at club nights? Your pricing only wins above about ₦5,000 a ticket. The marketing should follow the maths.
  5. Pick the name Then check the domain and the trademark before I change a single file.
  6. Show the processor fee openly? Costs you nothing, and would be the most honest pricing page in the market.
